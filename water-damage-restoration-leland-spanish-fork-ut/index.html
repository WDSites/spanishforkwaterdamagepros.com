Leland was organized as its own church ward on April 29, 1900, and a 1997 marker on West 900 South honors its pioneer settlers. A local history account places the old center about half a mile south of the Spanish Fork River and about 2.5 miles by road from downtown.

Today the name covers part of southwest Spanish Fork. If your address is outside city limits or you are not sure, call and we will confirm before anything is scheduled. See the main Spanish Fork water damage restoration page for the whole city.

Where Leland sits

Wikipedia describes Leland as an unincorporated community in southern Utah County that has been largely absorbed by Spanish Fork. The remainder falls inside the Benjamin census-designated place, which is outside the city.

The historical marker stands on West 900 South about 0.1 miles east of South 1700 West. The marker text calls the area a productive irrigated farming district whose roads and ditches were set by a survey in the 1860s. Official neighborhood boundaries are not published, so we describe the area by those landmarks.

A farming district with newer homes

The city's Residential Growth Map shows Leland Meadows with 259 homes. A neighborhood directory built on census tracts says many Leland homes were built in 2000 or later, with some from 1970 to 1999, and that most are owner occupied. That directory is a rough guide, not an official count.

We do not assume anything about basements, crawl spaces or plumbing materials in Leland, because the sources do not document them. A technician checks the actual structure and the source of the water on arrival.

Local facts that matter here

  • Leland's fields were watered by ditches laid out in the 1860s, according to the marker text. The city's pressurized irrigation system today typically runs from mid-April to mid-October, and the owner is responsible for the line past the first fitting after the meter.
  • The city's structural design criteria set a frost depth of 30 inches, which matters for lines and fittings outside the house.
  • The old center sits about half a mile south of the Spanish Fork River, and the city records river floods in 1983 and 1984. Distance alone does not decide a flood zone, and no FEMA finding for Leland as a whole is available to us.

In a residential area like this one, most calls start with an ordinary failure. A supply line splits, a water heater leaks, a dishwasher hose lets go or an air conditioner drain backs up. Roof leaks and heavy runoff add to the list in storm season.

First-hour safety steps

  1. If water is near the furnace, the panel or any outlet, do not walk through it.
  2. Close the main shut-off, but only when you can reach it without wading.
  3. Leave sewage or drain water alone, and keep children and pets away from it.
  4. Take pictures of the damage and of where the water seems to start.
  5. Raise dry furniture and boxes off the floor if that is safe.
  6. Large or electrical problems belong with professionals, and gas or fire with emergency services.

After the water is out

The crew extracts first and then sets air movers and dehumidifiers. Readings taken along the way are logged against the drying goals in ANSI/IICRC S500, so you can see when a wall or floor has reached dry. Materials that cannot be dried come out instead of being covered over.

Insurers often want to see photos, readings and an itemized scope, though coverage depends on the cause and the policy. The Utah Insurance Department notes that standard homeowners policies do not cover flood. Our emergency water removal page covers what happens first.

What Kills Mold Permanently? A Spanish Fork Homeowner Answer

Nothing kills mold for good, because spores are present in ordinary indoor air. What works is removing growth and controlling the moisture that lets it return.

☎ Call (385) 988-9410

By the Spanish Fork Water Damage Pros team. Updated October 25, 2026.

The honest answer: nothing is permanent

No product, spray or treatment kills mold permanently. Mold spores float through outdoor and indoor air everywhere. They settle on surfaces and wait for moisture.

That is why the useful question is not what kills mold but what keeps it from growing. Without moisture, growth stops. With moisture, it restarts, no matter what was applied before.

Killing is not the same as removing

A biocide can kill growth on a surface, but dead mold is still there. Dead and dormant mold can remain on materials, and it is physically removed in a proper remediation, not just treated.

Industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S520 focuses on removing contamination rather than relying on chemicals. HEPA vacuuming, damp wiping and removal of damaged porous material do the work.

A spray that leaves a clean-looking wall can look like success. Check whether the cause of moisture was found, because that is the real measure.

Porous versus nonporous materials

Glass, glazed tile, metal and sealed plastic are nonporous. Mold sits on top and can be cleaned off. Drywall, ceiling tile, insulation, carpet and unfinished wood are porous, and growth works into them.

Wood framing falls in between. Light surface growth on structural wood can often be cleaned, while deeply affected porous finishes are removed.

This is why remediation crews often cut out drywall rather than scrub it. The surface is the visible part, not the whole problem.

What remediation under S520 actually does

  1. Assess the extent and find the moisture source.
  2. Contain the affected area so spores do not spread to clean rooms.
  3. Run HEPA air scrubbers while work is underway.
  4. Remove contaminated porous materials and bag them.
  5. Clean remaining surfaces with HEPA vacuums and damp wiping.
  6. Dry the structure and verify moisture readings.

S520 is a published standard, and homeowners can ask any company whether its crews work to it. Our mold remediation service follows that approach, and the moisture side is covered by mitigation crews. We take calls from Provo and Payson as well as Spanish Fork.

Humidity habits that matter more than any spray

Controlling moisture is the lasting fix. Run bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans, fix leaks promptly, and dry wet items quickly. Keep indoor humidity low, and use a hygrometer to know what you actually have.

A dehumidifier in a basement helps when humidity runs high. Gutters and grading that send water away from the foundation help too.

These habits reduce the chance of growth. They do not guarantee that mold never appears, and no one can honestly promise that.

Pay attention to the places moisture hides: under sinks, behind washing machines, around window frames that collect condensation, and in closets against cold exterior walls. A quick monthly look catches small problems before they spread.

Testing and verification

After remediation, a company may offer testing to confirm cleanup. Results depend on the sampling method and the baseline of the building, so read them with the person who took them.

A visual check, moisture readings and a clean, dry structure are the practical proof. Health questions go to a physician.

If you only want to know whether a spot is worth worrying about, the useful details are size, material and moisture. A few small spots on sealed tile are a cleaning job. Spreading growth, soft drywall or a musty area that never dries are reasons to bring in a crew.

Remember that a clean-smelling room is not proof of a dry one. Moisture meters read the material itself, and that is the number that matters when you decide whether to repaint, close a wall or call for help.

Keep notes as you go: when you first saw it, where it is, and any recent leaks or spills. Those details help a crew find the source faster.

Spanish Fork note: spring runoff and soil moisture

The Spanish Fork River has a typical spring flow of 500 to 1,000 cfs, and it reached 2,770 cfs in 1983 and 3,700 cfs in 1984. Runoff season is when crawl spaces and basements take on moisture, so check them after the snow melts.

Spanish Fork's pressurized irrigation system typically runs mid-April to mid-October, and the owner is responsible for the line past the first fitting after the meter. A leak or overspray against the foundation adds moisture, so look at sprinklers near the house.

The Utah Insurance Department says standard homeowners policies do not cover flood. Mold that follows a flood is therefore a policy question, so ask your insurer before work begins.

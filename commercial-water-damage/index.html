Water damage in a commercial building follows the same physics as a house, but it changes how decisions get made. Floors are larger, ceilings are often tile or suspended, there may be a landlord, an owner and an insurer all involved, and the work has to be documented clearly. This page describes the water damage work we can do for a commercial property.

We handle the water side of the loss: extraction, drying, cleanup and a written record. We do not build out tenant space or repair mechanical systems. Related pages include emergency water removal for standing water and flood damage restoration for outside water.

Where commercial water losses start

Common starting points are a failed supply line, a burst or leaking roof area, a sprinkler or plumbing failure, an overflowing drain or a sewer backup. Water in a larger building tends to move along ceilings, behind walls and down through floors, so one failure can reach several areas.

The Census Bureau counted 42,602 residents in Spanish Fork in 2020 and estimates 48,837 for 2025. A city that size has a wide mix of buildings, and every one of them has plumbing, a roof and drains that can fail.

The city's floodplain map uses FEMA zones AE, AE Floodway, A and X, and these apply to any parcel, not only homes. A property owner can look up an address at the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. The city also notes the Spanish Fork River floods of 1983 and 1984 in its flood information.

First-hour safety steps

  1. Close the water supply to the affected area or the building, if you know where the valve is.
  2. Keep people and customers out of wet areas and away from sagging ceilings.
  3. Turn off power to wet areas only if the panel is dry and reachable. If water is touching electrical equipment, call the electric utility or 911.
  4. Move records, electronics and stock out of the water and off the floor.
  5. Photograph the damage and note the time it was found.
  6. Call the emergency line, then your property manager or insurer.

Large losses need a clear chain of contact

Tell the dispatcher who has authority to approve work and who holds the insurance policy. That keeps decisions from stalling while water spreads.

How our crew handles a commercial loss

The crew starts with a walkthrough, using a moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera to map the wet areas. Standing water is pumped or extracted, and wet materials that cannot dry, such as soaked ceiling tile or carpet pad, are removed.

Drying uses air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, with injection drying for walls and floor drying mats for hard surfaces. The crew logs equipment and readings each visit against the goals described in ANSI/IICRC S500.

When the readings meet the goal, the equipment comes out and the area is cleaned. If contaminated water or mold is involved, those steps are scoped separately. Nearby buildings in Provo and Payson get the same process.

Tell the dispatcher who approves work and who holds the policy, and we will take it from there.

What we can and cannot do

  • We can extract water, dry structures and clean up after a water loss
  • We can document readings, equipment and photographs for your records and your insurer
  • We can coordinate timing with your plumber, roofer or property manager
  • We do not repair plumbing, roofs or mechanical systems
  • We do not promise a specific arrival time or a specific outcome before we see the loss
  • We explain scope and price before work starts

What changes the cost of a commercial loss

  • How large the wet area is and how many floors it reached
  • What materials are wet, such as ceiling tile, carpet tile, drywall or concrete
  • Whether the water is clean or contaminated
  • How much of the building must stay open while we work
  • How many days of drying equipment and monitoring are needed
  • Whether after-hours work or phased access is required

Insurance and documentation

Commercial property policies vary widely, and a tenant and an owner may each have a policy that applies. Ask your agent which one responds to a given loss. The Utah Insurance Department's guidance, which is written for homeowners, still gives a useful checklist: call the insurer early, take photographs or video, keep receipts and avoid permanent repairs until the insurer has inspected.

We keep daily moisture readings, equipment logs and photographs so that the claim file shows what was wet, what was removed and when the structure dried.

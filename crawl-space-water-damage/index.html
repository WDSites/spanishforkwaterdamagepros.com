Emergency Crawl Space Water Damage in Spanish Fork, UT

Water under a house is out of sight and easy to ignore. Our 24/7 emergency line covers pumping, drying and cleanup in crawl spaces after a leak, a flood or groundwater.

A crawl space is a low, dark space under the first floor, and it is where a lot of plumbing, wiring and insulation live. When water gets in, it soaks the soil, the insulation and the underside of the floor, and the damage shows up later as cupped floors, a musty smell or mold on the joists.

Not every Spanish Fork house has a crawl space, and many are built over basements instead. If yours does, water here needs to be handled with care. If the water came from a river or runoff, flood damage restoration explains the larger picture. If it came from a pipe, burst pipe water damage is the closer match.

How water gets into crawl spaces here

Spanish Fork's flood history sets the background. The city's flood information records Spanish Fork River floods in 1983 and 1984, with flows of 2,770 and 3,700 cubic feet per second, compared with a typical spring flow of 500 to 1,000. High water and rising groundwater push into low spaces under houses.

Day to day, the more common sources are smaller. A split pipe under the floor, a poorly aimed sprinkler, a window well that overflows, or a gutter downspout that dumps beside the foundation can all feed a crawl space. Spanish Fork City's pressurized irrigation system typically runs from mid-April to mid-October, so those months bring extra water near foundations.

The city's structural design criteria set the frost depth at 30 inches, which is why foundation walls reach deep. A foundation can also have gaps around pipes and vents where water finds its way in.

First-hour safety steps

  1. Do not enter a crawl space with standing water if power cables or equipment are in it.
  2. Turn off power to the crawl space circuit at the panel if you can reach it dry.
  3. Close the main water valve if a pipe is the source.
  4. Do not go into a tight space alone, and wear a mask and gloves if you must look in.
  5. Photograph what you can see from the access and note where the water is coming from.
  6. Call the emergency line and your insurer.

Contamination and confined spaces

Floodwater or sewage in a crawl space is contaminated and can carry hazards. Leave it to a trained crew, and keep children and pets away from the access.

How our crew handles a crawl space loss

The first step is a safe look. The crew checks the access, the water depth and any electrical hazards, then pumps standing water with submersible pumps and a hose line to a proper discharge location.

Wet insulation that has soaked through is removed. A moisture meter and thermal imaging camera check the joists, subfloor and sill plate, and the vapor barrier is pulled back where needed. Dry sections are left alone.

Drying in a crawl space is harder than in a room. Air movers and dehumidifiers are set up with ventilation or ducting, and the crew logs readings against the goals in ANSI/IICRC S500. If the wood shows mold, mold remediation is a separate step with containment.

Insurance and crawl space water

Whether a loss is covered depends on where the water came from. A burst pipe in the crawl space is a plumbing loss. Surface water, groundwater or river overflow is usually a flood loss.

The Utah Insurance Department says standard homeowners policies do not cover flood, and flood insurance typically has a waiting period of about 30 days. The city's floodplain manager recommends flood insurance, noting that a property in the 1 percent annual chance zone faces about a 25 percent chance of flooding over a 30 year mortgage. Check the FEMA Flood Map Service Center for your address.

Mistakes and prevention

  • Ignoring a musty smell, hoping it will pass
  • Closing the access and walking away from standing water
  • Letting downspouts dump at the foundation
  • Leaving wet insulation to sag against the floor
  • Fixing the floor above without drying the space below
